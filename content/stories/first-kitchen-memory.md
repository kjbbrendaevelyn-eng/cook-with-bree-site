I must have been five or six. My grandmother had this old step stool — green paint chipped at the edges — that she kept tucked beside the kitchen counter. On slow Saturday mornings, she'd pull it out and I'd climb up, my chin barely clearing the counter, and we'd bake together.

The first thing she ever taught me to make was banana bread. Not because it was easy (though it is), but because, as she told me, "You can't rush a banana. You have to wait until it's ready." I didn't understand what she meant then. I just wanted to lick the batter off the spoon.

She'd let me mash the bananas with a fork — the satisfying squish of overripe fruit collapsing under my small hands. She'd measure the flour and I'd dump it in, usually sending a puff of white into the air. She never scolded me for the mess.

What I remember most isn't the recipe, though I still make it exactly her way. It's the smell — warm banana and cinnamon filling her tiny kitchen — and the way she'd squeeze my shoulder and say, "Good job, sweetheart," like I'd accomplished something extraordinary.

Because in her kitchen, I had.

That's the thing about food memories. They aren't really about the food at all. They're about the person who stood beside you, the patience they showed, the love they folded into every step without ever saying the word.

I started Cook with Bree because I wanted a place to hold those moments — and to create new ones. Every recipe here carries a piece of someone I love, or a night worth remembering, or a lesson learned the hard way (usually involving too much salt).

This blog is my step stool. I hope it helps you climb up to your own counter and make something that matters.
