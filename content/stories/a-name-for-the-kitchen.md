Lecture halls taught me to think. Runways taught me I deserved to be seen. The kitchen is where those two lessons sat down together and asked for a name.

It started smaller than a name. A hostel burner. A saucepan. Rice and beans on the weeks that had to stretch, an egg with onion and tomato when I needed dinner to feel like care. I would come back from a casting or a lecture with my heels in one bag and my notes in the other, and the first thing that made the room mine again was food. Friends followed the smell down the corridor and stayed. There was no dining table. There was a pot, and a door that ended up open.

The trips kept filling the margins. Saffron and slow rice from **Dubai**. Pilau and sukuma wiki from **Kenya**. Berbere, spiced butter, and one big shared plate from **Ethiopia**. A braai in **South Africa** that turned a meal into a gathering. I wrote the techniques on the backs of call sheets, then cooked them again on that same small burner, testing whether a recipe could survive the trip home. Most of them could. The ones that could were the ones I trusted.

When I started **Cook with Bree**, I was putting those trusted things in a place other people could find.

The business degree, which had felt like a timetable I was racing, turned into the work of building something. A name. A page for each recipe. A reason to show up on a quiet evening when the only person who would notice was me. In class we had talked about branding and knowing your value. At the stove I already knew the value. It was a full plate, a warm room, someone fed. The site was just the honest way to say so.

Modeling had taught me the part I used to avoid. For years I had made myself small and useful, the girl who coped. On a set, the work was to stand where I could be seen. Publishing a recipe asked for the same courage in a different light. Here is the flour on the counter. Here is the meal I know by heart. Here is the story that comes with it. The photo can be my own kitchen. The words can sound like me.

My working days are still long. Spreadsheets, messages, a mind that stays loud until my hands have something to do. Cooking is the pause that actually arrives. Chopping steadies me. Garlic in oil changes the job of the evening. And when a friend sits down to a bowl I made for a hard week — lemon garlic pasta, no speech required — I remember the hostel corridor, and the way a smell can call people in.

I cook with the habits I learned twice. Once in my mother's kitchen, where leftovers became the next meal. Again in the compounds where farmers showed me that whey, buttermilk, and the brown bits of ghee were never waste. My father's Friday stories are in the way I want a meal to take its time. **Salma** is in the memory of two girls who loved the same room in completely different ways.

Cook with Bree is that room, with the door left the way the hostel door used to be.

I still miss a deadline now and then. I still eat standing up on the tired nights. The girl who taught herself to survive is still the one at the stove. She has a name for the kitchen now, and a chair for whoever needs feeding.
