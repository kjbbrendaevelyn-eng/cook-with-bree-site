Once the kitchen had a name, I had to decide who the chair was for.

I already knew how to feed the people who could smell the pot. In the hostel, friends followed the corridor and sat wherever there was space. A bowl, a spoon, the food still hot. That kind of cooking happens in a room you are already in. You can watch their face. You can add a pinch of salt while they are still talking.

The room is fuller now.

I am married, and there are three children at this table. **Jordan** is fifteen. **Elli** is two. **Riley** is six months. Dinner has to hold all of them in one evening: a real plate for Jordan, a small portion Elli can manage, and a kitchen calm enough that Riley can be in it while I cook. My spouse shares that table with me. The chair I used to set for whoever wandered in has their names on it.

**Cook with Bree** still asks me to cook for someone who will never walk into this house.

That meant writing the meal as if my hands would not be there to finish it. How much flour, in cups and in grams, because a guess only works when I am the one guessing. How the dough should feel. How the oil should sound when it is ready. What to do when it cracks, and what to do when it smears. I needed those lines when I was learning alone, on a single burner, with a saucepan and a tired evening. So I put them on the page. I write them now with this table in mind. A recipe that can feed a teenager, a toddler, and a baby in the same hour is a recipe someone else can trust on a tired night.

The people I can still see come first. Jordan, Elli, and Riley, and the person I married. A friend still has a place here too: a hard week, lemon garlic pasta set down while it was hot. She is still talking about that bowl. The recipe has to offer the same thing when I am not in the chair across from her: a warm plate, and the sense that someone meant it.

Then the page has to travel.

Someone in their own kitchen, with their own pot, reading what I wrote and deciding to trust it. I picture them the way I stood at my mother's stove, and later at that hostel burner: wanting the food to turn out, willing to try. The small sentences are the hospitality. Heat the pan. Salt the water. Rest the dough until it rolls. Eat it warm. If the middle is still pale, give it another minute. A recipe is a door left open in words.

Some nights I still eat standing up. The workday runs long. Spreadsheets, messages, a mind that will not sit down. Elli is close to the counter. Riley needs holding. Jordan comes in when the food is ready. Cooking on those nights is the same offer I make on the page. This house is worth feeding.

My father's stories took a whole Friday because the point was the people on the floor, **Salma** and me, waiting for the ending. A recipe is a shorter story with the same hope. It begins in one kitchen. Someone else gets to finish it in theirs. I want Jordan, Elli, and Riley to have both: a table that takes its time, and a page they can cook from when they have kitchens of their own.

The name on the door is still Cook with Bree. The chairs are for Jordan, Elli, Riley, and the person I share them with. One more stays open for the person who will cook the meal when this house is already asleep.
