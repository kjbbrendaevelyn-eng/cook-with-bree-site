Once the kitchen had a name, I had to decide who the chair was for.

I already knew how to feed the people who could smell the pot. In the hostel, friends followed the corridor and sat wherever there was space. A bowl, a spoon, the food still hot. That kind of cooking happens in a room you are already in. You can watch their face. You can add a pinch of salt while they are still talking.

**Cook with Bree** asked me to cook for someone who would never walk down that corridor.

That meant writing the meal as if my hands would not be there to finish it. How much flour, in cups and in grams, because a guess only works when I am the one guessing. How the dough should feel. How the oil should sound when it is ready. What to do when it cracks, and what to do when it smears. I needed those lines when I was learning alone, on a single burner, with a saucepan and a tired evening. So I put them on the page.

The people I can still see come first. A friend, a hard week, lemon garlic pasta set down while it was hot. She is still talking about that bowl. The recipe has to offer the same thing when I am not in the chair across from her: a warm plate, and the sense that someone meant it.

Then the page has to travel.

Someone in their own kitchen, with their own pot, reading what I wrote and deciding to trust it. I picture them the way I stood at my mother's stove, and later at that hostel burner: wanting the food to turn out, willing to try. The small sentences are the hospitality. Heat the pan. Salt the water. Rest the dough until it rolls. Eat it warm. If the middle is still pale, give it another minute. A recipe is a door left open in words.

Some nights the chair is for me. The workday runs long. Spreadsheets, messages, a mind that will not sit down. I still chop the onion. I still eat standing up. Cooking for myself on those nights is the same offer I make on the page. You are worth feeding, even on the evening when nobody else is coming over.

My father's stories took a whole Friday because the point was the people on the floor, **Salma** and me, waiting for the ending. A recipe is a shorter story with the same hope. It begins in one kitchen. Someone else gets to finish it in theirs.

The name on the door is still Cook with Bree. The chair is still there. These days I set it for the friend across from me, and for the person who will cook the meal when I am already asleep.
