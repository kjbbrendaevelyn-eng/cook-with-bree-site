I started university and my modeling career in the same season, which is a polite way of saying I had no idea what I was doing in two places at once.

The scholarship I had chased through all those essays finally came through. After the years with my cousin, after learning to cook and clean and work between classes, walking onto campus felt unreal, like the future I had been writing towards had suddenly agreed to let me in. I was there to study **business**. I had a timetable, a hostel room, and a list of reading about accounting, marketing, and economics that I didn't understand yet.

And then, almost at the same time, modeling found me.

My very first photoshoot was on a **beach in Kenya**.

I remember the sand burning under my feet, the wind refusing to leave my hair alone, and the ocean behind me so wide and bright it felt like it was showing off. I had no idea how to pose. I didn't know where to put my hands, when to smile, or how to look natural while a whole crew stood around waiting for me to figure it out. The photographer kept saying "relax," and I kept thinking, *I have never been less relaxed in my life.* But somewhere between the waves and the nerves, I stopped thinking so hard and just stood there in the light. When I saw the pictures later, I barely recognised the girl in them. She looked free.

I came back to campus with sun on my skin and a secret: maybe I could do this.

I won't pretend it was glamorous after that. It was early morning castings before lectures, waiting in hallways with girls who all seemed to know the rules already. It was learning how to walk, how to stand, how to hold my face still while a photographer said "again" for the hundredth time. It was carrying my heels in a bag and my notes in the other, changing in bathrooms between a shoot and a seminar, and hoping nobody could tell I had run the whole way there.

Some days I felt like two different people.

In the lecture hall I was a business student trying to keep up, scribbling notes on balance sheets and business plans and praying I'd understood the assignment. On set I was supposed to be confident, still, beautiful on command. Nobody in either room knew about the other. Nobody knew I was the girl who had buried both her parents and had been taking care of herself ever since. I carried that quietly into both rooms.

Modeling paid in ways that mattered. It helped with rent, books, and food at a time when I had nobody to call for pocket money. It also taught me something I didn't expect: how to take up space. For years I had made myself small and useful, the one who coped, who managed, who didn't need much. In front of a camera, being small wasn't the job. Standing tall was.

Funny enough, my two worlds started to talk to each other. In class we learned about branding, pricing, and knowing your value. On set I was living it, learning that I was the product, the brand, and the one who had to negotiate for myself. My business lectures stopped feeling like theory. They felt like survival skills with better vocabulary.

And through all of it, I kept cooking.

My hostel kitchen was a single burner and a small saucepan, but it was mine. On late nights after a shoot, when I was too tired to read another page, I'd make something simple: rice with whatever beans I could afford, an egg fried with onion and tomato, chapati when I had the energy. Friends would wander in following the smell, and suddenly a cramped hostel room felt like a home. Cooking kept me grounded when everything else asked me to be someone new.

The more I travelled, the bigger my kitchen got, even when the stove stayed the same size. Trips to **Dubai**, **Kenya**, **Ethiopia**, and **South Africa** sent me home with new techniques and recipes tucked away like souvenirs. In Dubai I fell for warm spices, saffron, and slow-cooked rice. In Kenya I learned from cooks who made pilau and sukuma wiki taste like a celebration. Ethiopia taught me about spiced butter, berbere, and sharing one big plate with everyone around it. South Africa showed me how a braai turns a meal into a gathering. I asked questions everywhere I went, watched hands that knew what they were doing, and wrote notes on the back of call sheets. Every trip changed the way I cooked when I got back.

Looking back, I don't know how I balanced it all, and some weeks I didn't. I missed deadlines and castings. I cried in bathroom stalls and ate dinner standing up. But I also learned that the same girl who taught herself to survive could teach herself to shine. Lecture halls taught me to think. Runways taught me to believe I deserved to be seen.

Both of them made me who I am in the kitchen today: someone who can work hard, show up, and still make time to feed the people around her. And somewhere in the middle of building Cook with Bree, I keep catching myself using that business degree too.
